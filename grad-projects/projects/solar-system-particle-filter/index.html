Solar System: finding a lost satellite

A satellite is lost somewhere in a solar system. The only thing it can measure is how strongly gravity is pulling on it. A particle filter turns that single number, read over and over, into a position.

The problem

The satellite knows the commands it was given (how far to move and how much to turn), but not where it started. Each step it gets one noisy reading: the total magnitude of gravitational pull from the sun and planets. A single reading only says "you're somewhere on this ring" (the purple haze). As the satellite and planets move, the rings shift, and only one place stays consistent with every reading.

Part two of the project: once localized, compute the bearing from the satellite to the home planet to send an SOS, this time using how illuminated each planet looks from the satellite.

My approach

  • Scatter: 20,000 particles spread uniformly over ±4 AU, each facing along a circular orbit.
  • Weigh: each particle's weight is a Gaussian likelihood comparing the measured gravity with what that particle would feel at its position.
  • Resample: low-variance (systematic) resampling. I shrank the population as the belief tightened: halving it early, then keeping a steady fraction. That kept each step fast without losing accuracy.
  • Fuzz: small random jitter after resampling, so particles don't collapse onto identical copies.
  • Move: every particle applies the same distance and steering command using a bicycle-style motion model.
  • Estimate: the mean of the surviving particles.

For the SOS part, the same filter ran on the vector of planet-illumination readings, and the bearing was the angle from the estimate to the home planet's predicted next position.

What to try

Drop the particle count to 1,000 and hit "New system" a few times. Sometimes the cloud locks onto the wrong ring. That's the classic particle-filter trade-off between speed and robustness.
