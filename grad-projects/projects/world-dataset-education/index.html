World DataSet: what predicts a country's education?

Public data about the world is scattered across dozens of portals, each with its own country names, years and formats. My team spent over a hundred hours merging eight of them into two clean, documented datasets, then used them to ask one question: of all the things we can measure about a country, which ones go with its children staying in school longer?

The 30-second version

  • Built: two curated datasets. A 20-year panel (198 countries × 1990–2020, 239 indicators: health, deaths by cause, refugees, displacement, disasters, World Bank development indicators) and a recent cross-section (71 countries, 27 variables: educational mobility, gender equality, political stability, income, temperature).
  • Asked: what predicts the mean years of schooling of a country's children?
  • Found: parents' schooling dominates (r = 0.86). Political stability, income and education spending all help. Hotter countries school their children less (r = −0.58), which is almost certainly geography standing in for wealth, not heat itself.
  • Modelled: a linear regression on eight variables explains 83% of the variance; each extra year of parental schooling is worth about 0.67 years for the child. PCA compresses the nine variables to three axes that carry 85% of the variation.
Background

Two datasets, eight sources

This was a group project for a data-science course in autumn 2022. The deliverable was not a model but a dataset: something other people could download and analyse without repeating the cleaning. We published it on GitHub with a README documenting every column, every source and every join, and then wrote our own analysis on top of it as a worked example.

The problem with world data is that every organisation publishes its own slice. The World Bank has development indicators by country and year. Our World in Data has causes of death. The UNHCR counts refugees; the IDMC counts people displaced inside their own country by conflict and by disasters. None of them agree on country names, which years exist, or how wide the table should be. Joining them is the work.

Part 1

Curating the data

The 20-year panel was the heavy lift. Its backbone is the World Bank's World Development Indicators, which arrives as one long table: a row per country per indicator with a column per year. Everything else is a wide table with a row per country-year. Getting them to meet took a reshape, a lot of outer joins and a rule for throwing columns away.

Pick one key and stick to it

Every table was coerced to the same three columns: Country, Code (ISO-3) and Year. Country names differ between sources ("Viet Nam", "Vietnam"), so the ISO code carried the joins and the name came along for display.

Merge the country-year tables

Child mortality joined health spending, then refugees, then causes of death, then the disaster columns we kept (economic damage as a share of GDP, death and injury rates, people affected per 100,000). All outer joins, so a country missing from one source isn't dropped from the rest.

Under the hood 
health = child_mortality.merge(health_spend, on=["Year", "Code", "Country"], how="outer")
health = health.merge(refugees, on=["Year", "Code", "Country"], how="outer")
health = health.merge(causes_of_death, on=["Year", "Code", "Country"], how="outer")
health = health.merge(disasters[keep_cols], on=["Year", "Country"], how="outer")
health = health[~health.Year.isin(years_before_1990)]   # some sources go back to 1900

Pivot the World Bank table

WDI has one row per (country, indicator) and 60 year-columns. I pivoted it so years became rows and indicators became columns, then stacked the country code back in. That turns a 1,400-indicator catalogue into a wide country-year table that joins like the others.

Under the hood 
wdi = wdi.pivot(index=["Year"], columns=["Country Code", "Indicator Name"], values=["Value"])
wdi = wdi.stack(level=["Country Code"]).reset_index()

missing = wdi.isnull().sum() / len(wdi)
wdi = wdi.drop(missing[missing > 0.18].index, axis=1)     # keep indicators ≥82% populated

The 18% threshold is the single most consequential line in the project. Loosen it and you keep hundreds of indicators that are empty for most of the world; tighten it and you lose the interesting ones. We picked the value that kept the panel usable for a regression without imputation.

Join, filter, publish

One last outer join of the health block onto the pivoted WDI on (Year, Code), drop rows with no country code (regional aggregates like "World" and "Sub-Saharan Africa"), and write three files: the panel, a per-country-and-year grouping for trend reading, and a summary-statistics table for every column.

Build the recent snapshot

The second dataset is narrower and newer. Its spine is the World Bank's Global Database on Intergenerational Mobility: for each country, the mean years of schooling of parents and of their children, for sons, daughters and all, against mothers, fathers and both. We joined on the Global Gender Gap sub-indices, the Worldwide Governance Indicators' political-stability estimate, a country-level IQ / income / education-spending table and daily maximum temperature, then attached ISO sub-regions so results could be grouped geographically.

Why publish the dataset, not just the analysis

Most course projects end at a notebook nobody can rerun. Shipping the merged CSVs with a column-by-column README means the next person starts from the clean table, and it forced us to be explicit about every choice: which join, which threshold, which years were cut. The README is longer than the analysis.

Part 2

Exploring education

With the recent snapshot in hand, the analysis question was: what goes with children getting more schooling? The outcome variable is MEANc, the mean years of schooling of the child generation, taken for all children against the average of both parents, one row per country.

The temperature puzzle

The surprise in the correlation matrix was daily maximum temperature: hotter countries school their children less, at r = −0.58. Plotting it by income group explains most of it.

Temperature is standing in for geography, and geography is tangled up with colonial history, disease burden and income. The data can't separate those, and the project report said so: the correlation is real, the causal story is not something a cross-section of 68 countries can tell you. It became the main example in our write-up of why "correlated with" and "caused by" are different sentences.

A note on the IQ column

One source table carried a "national average IQ" figure alongside income and education spending. It was in the dataset, so it was in the correlation matrix (r = 0.69 with schooling). Country-level IQ estimates are contested in the research literature, and in this data they move almost in lockstep with schooling itself, so they add little beyond what parents' education already says. If I rebuilt the dataset today I would leave that column out.

Part 3

PCA and regression

Nine correlated variables is too many to reason about one at a time, so I did two things: compressed them with principal component analysis to see how many independent "axes" the countries really vary along, and fitted a linear regression to put numbers on each variable's contribution.

Standardise, then PCA

Income is in dollars (variance 2 × 10⁸), schooling in years (variance ~15). Without centring and scaling, PCA would just find "income". After standardising each column, sklearn.decomposition.PCA on the nine variables gave the variance shares in Figure 5.

Read the loadings

PC1 (66%) is a general "development" axis: every variable loads the same direction except temperature, which loads opposite. PC2 (10%) separates income and education spending from the rest, picking out rich countries whose schooling is lower than their money predicts. PC3 (8%) is parents' and children's schooling together, against gender equality.

Under the hood

Loadings are the weights of each original variable in a component. I plotted them as a line per component with a zero line, which makes the sign pattern obvious at a glance: PC1 is all-one-sign-except-temperature. Projecting the countries onto PC pairs and flagging the far tail (sum of two scores above a cutoff) surfaced the outliers worth a second look, in particular the handful of countries far out on the PC1-plus-PC3 axis.

Fit the regression

Ordinary least squares of children's schooling on eight predictors (parents' schooling, gender equality index, economic-participation equality, political stability, IQ, income, education spending, temperature), with an intercept column added by hand.

Under the hood 
X = add_dummy_feature(df[predictors], value=1)   # intercept column
mlr = LinearRegression(fit_intercept=False).fit(X, df.MEANc)
r2_score(df.MEANc, mlr.predict(X))                   # → 0.826

I fitted the same model six times, once per parent–child pairing (sons on fathers, daughters on mothers and so on). R² sat between 0.80 and 0.83 every time, which says the relationship is stable rather than an artefact of one subset.

Parental schooling is the coefficient that matters: 0.67 with a standard error of 0.03, so a child in a country where parents averaged ten years of school is predicted to get about 6.7 more years than one where parents averaged zero, holding everything else fixed. Political stability and the gender index were both clearly non-zero. Education spending per inhabitant was the one predictor whose coefficient sat within a standard error of zero once income was in the model: spending and income are too correlated for the regression to tell them apart.

Discussion

What it adds up to

  • Education is inherited. Across 68 countries the single best predictor of how long children stay in school is how long their parents did. Everything else is second order.
  • Stability and money help, spending alone doesn't show. Political stability and income each carry weight; education expenditure per head adds nothing the model can separate from income.
  • The world varies along about three axes. Nine development indicators collapse to a general development axis, an income-versus-schooling axis and a schooling-versus-gender-equality axis.
  • From the 20-year panel: 73% of countries increased per-capita CO₂ emissions between 1970 and 2016, but only 59% did between 2006 and 2016, with several of the biggest emitters trending down.
If I did it again

Drop the IQ column, add a year dimension to the recent dataset so the regression could use country fixed effects instead of a single cross-section, and replace the 18% missingness cutoff with a documented per-indicator decision. And write the README first, not last.

Looking back

What it taught me

  1. Cleaning is the project. The analysis took an afternoon; the dataset took a hundred hours. Every interesting result rested on a join decision made weeks earlier.
  2. Outer joins, then look at the holes. Inner joins silently shrink the world to the countries every source agrees on. Outer joins keep everyone and make the missingness visible, which is where the real decisions are.
  3. Scale before PCA, always. The first unscaled run returned a single component that was just "income in dollars". Standardising turned it into something interpretable.
  4. Say what the data can't say. The temperature correlation was the most tempting finding and the least causal. Writing the limitation into the README felt like a weakness and turned out to be the most useful paragraph in it.
Reference

Key terms

WDI
World Development Indicators, the World Bank's catalogue of ~1,400 country-year statistics.
GDIM
Global Database on Intergenerational Mobility: parents' and children's schooling from surveys in 150+ economies, 2008–2016.
panel data
The same units (countries) observed at many times (years). Lets you compare a country with itself.
outer join
A merge that keeps rows from both tables even when the other has no match, filling the gap with NaN.
pivot / stack
Reshaping a table between long (one value per row) and wide (one column per variable) forms.
PCA
Principal component analysis: rotates correlated variables into uncorrelated axes ordered by how much variance each carries.
loading
The weight of an original variable in a principal component; its sign and size say what the component means.
R²
Share of the outcome's variance a regression explains. 0.83 here.
standard error
Uncertainty of a coefficient. A coefficient within one SE of zero can't be distinguished from no effect.
