Cryptography: when correct maths meets bad choices

RSA is mathematically sound, yet real systems keep breaking it. I implemented RSA from first principles and then the classic attacks on weak deployments, which made it very clear that crypto fails in the details, not the theory.

The 30-second version

  • Did: implemented RSA from first principles in Python and then the classic attacks on weak deployments, recovering private keys and messages from setups that look secure on paper.
  • Attacks: keys too small to resist factoring; primes shared between different keys (broken by a single GCD); primes too close together (Fermat's method); and tiny public exponents used without padding.
  • Takeaway: none of these break RSA's mathematics. They break the choices around it: key size, randomness and padding. That is where real cryptographic failures live.
Background

RSA from first principles

Pick two primes

Choose large random primes p and q and keep them secret.

Make the modulus

Publish n = p × q. Everyone sees n; only you know its factors.

Choose exponents

Pick a public exponent e (commonly 65,537) and compute the private exponent d so that e × d ≡ 1 (mod (p−1)(q−1)). Computing d requires knowing p and q.

Encrypt and decrypt

Encryption is c = me mod n. Decryption is m = cd mod n. The security rests entirely on factoring n being infeasible.

The attacks

Four ways good maths meets bad choices

1 · Keys that are too small

If n is short enough, ordinary factoring algorithms recover p and q in seconds. Once you have them, computing d is trivial and every message is readable.

Fix: 2048-bit keys or larger, or elliptic-curve cryptography.

2 · Primes shared between keys

Two different public keys that accidentally share a prime fall to one greatest-common-divisor computation: gcd(n₁, n₂) = the shared prime. This has really happened at internet scale, with devices that generated keys from poor randomness at boot.

Fix: cryptographically secure randomness, properly seeded.

3 · Primes too close together

If p and q are nearly equal, both are close to √n. Fermat's method searches outward from √n and finds them almost immediately, no matter how large n is.

Fix: generate p and q independently with a library that enforces a minimum gap.

4 · Small exponents, no padding

With a tiny e (like 3) and a short message, me may never wrap around n, so the "ciphertext" is just an ordinary cube and an integer cube root recovers the message.

Fix: never use "textbook" RSA; always use OAEP padding.

Defence

How to do it properly

Use vetted libraries
Let a well-reviewed library generate keys and handle padding; never hand-roll the primitives.
Modern parameters
RSA-2048 or larger, or move to elliptic-curve schemes (e.g. X25519, Ed25519).
Always pad
OAEP for encryption and PSS for signatures; textbook RSA is never safe.
Good randomness
Keys must come from a cryptographically secure RNG with enough entropy, especially on embedded devices.
Plan for rotation
Assume keys will one day need replacing, and make that routine.
Looking back

What it taught me

  1. Security lives in the details. The algorithm was sound every time; parameters and randomness were not.
  2. Small maths, big consequences. A one-line GCD broke keys that looked strong.
  3. Understanding attacks is why you trust libraries. Seeing how easily these fail is exactly why I would never implement crypto by hand in production.
Reference

Key terms

RSA
Public-key cryptosystem resting on the difficulty of factoring large numbers.
modulus n
The public product p × q.
φ(n)
(p−1)(q−1); needed to compute the private exponent.
modular inverse
The d with e × d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)).
GCD
Greatest common divisor; exposes a prime shared by two moduli.
Fermat factorisation
Finds p and q quickly when they are close to √n.
OAEP
Randomised padding that makes textbook attacks fail.
