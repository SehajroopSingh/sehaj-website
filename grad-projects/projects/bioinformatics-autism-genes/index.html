Autism genes: from a database to a network

Roughly a thousand human genes have been linked to autism spectrum disorder, with very different amounts of evidence behind each. I took the public SFARI gene list and asked three questions of it with Python and a few web tools: what is the research literature saying about these genes, what do the genes actually do, and do their proteins work together?

The 30-second version

  • The data: the SFARI Gene database, a curated list of autism-linked genes, each scored 1 (strong evidence), 2 or 3 (weaker). In my download: 215, 690 and 91 genes.
  • Part 1, literature: for the most-reported genes I queried PubMed by script, year by year since 2000. Research on the top gene, SHANK3, went from a paper or two a year to about fifty.
  • Part 2, function: I mapped every gene to its Gene Ontology annotations and ran PANTHER classification. At the broad level the three evidence tiers look alike: the lists differ in how sure we are, not in what the genes do.
  • Part 3, network: the strong-evidence genes form a protein-interaction network with 1,555 interactions where ~460 would be expected by chance. Clustering splits it into a synapse group and a chromatin/gene-regulation group, the two big themes in autism genetics.
Background

The question and the data

This was the final project for a graduate bioinformatics course in autumn 2022. The brief was open: pick a disease, find its genes, and use the tools from the course (Biopython, Gene Ontology, enrichment analysis, interaction networks) to learn something about them. I picked autism spectrum disorder because the genetics are unusually rich and unusually messy: hundreds of genes, each contributing a little, and a research literature that is still arguing about which pathways matter.

The starting point was the SFARI Gene database. SFARI curates every human gene with published evidence for an autism link and gives each a gene score: 1 means the gene is clearly implicated, typically by three or more independent de novo gene-disrupting mutations; 2 means strong but thinner evidence; 3 means suggestive, often a single report. The score is the thread that runs through the whole project: at each step I asked whether the strong-evidence genes behave differently from the weaker ones.

Part 1

What the literature says

Before analysing the genes themselves I wanted to know where the weight of published evidence sits, and whether it is moving. SFARI records how many reports each gene has; PubMed can tell you how many papers mention a gene together with autism. Comparing the two, and slicing the second by year, shows which genes the field is converging on.

Load the list and count by score

One pandas.read_csv straight from the SFARI export URL, then value_counts on the gene-score column. Score 2 is by far the largest tier; score 1 is under a third of it; score 3 has fewer than a hundred genes.

Find the most-reported genes in each tier

Sort each tier by SFARI's number of reports and keep the top five. For score 1 these are SHANK3, MECP2, NRXN1, SCN2A and SCN1A: a synaptic scaffold, a chromatin reader, a synaptic adhesion molecule and two sodium channels.

Ask PubMed about each one

Biopython's Entrez.egquery runs a search across all NCBI databases and returns the hit count per database. I queried "Autism Spectrum Disorder" + gene symbol for each of the fifteen genes and read off the PubMed count.

Under the hood

Entrez is NCBI's query layer; every database (PubMed, Nucleotide, Gene, Taxonomy…) sits behind it. egquery is the cheapest call because it returns only counts, which is all a literature survey needs. NCBI asks that you set Entrez.email so they can contact you if a script misbehaves, and limits you to a few requests per second, so the loops below pause between calls.

from Bio import Entrez
Entrez.email = "you@example.com"

def pubmed_count(term):
    record = Entrez.read(Entrez.egquery(term=term))
    for row in record["eGQueryResult"]:
        if row["DbName"] == "pubmed":
            return int(row["Count"])

pubmed_count('"Autism Spectrum Disorder" SHANK3')   # → 280 in Nov 2022

Slice it by year

Running the same query with a year prefix, for every year from 2000 to 2022 and every gene, gives a genes × years table. I built it as a DataFrame and plotted it as a stacked bar chart so the growth of the whole field and the share of each gene are visible at once.

Under the hood

That is 5 genes × 23 years = 115 queries per tier. I kept each gene's loop in its own cell so a throttled request would not wipe out the whole table, then assembled the rows into one DataFrame and called df.plot.bar(stacked=True). The chart below is the same data redrawn for this page.

Why this matters

A literature trend is weak evidence on its own (attention breeds attention), but it is useful context for everything that follows. When the network in Part 3 puts SHANK3's partners at the centre of a cluster, it helps to know that this is also where the field has been looking hardest.

Part 2

What the genes do

Gene names tell you nothing about function. The Gene Ontology (GO) does: it is a controlled vocabulary of terms such as "plasma membrane" or "nervous system development", and NCBI publishes a table, gene2go, that links every gene ID to the terms it has been annotated with. Getting from SFARI's list to that table was mostly a problem of identifiers.

Map every gene to an NCBI ID

SFARI identifies genes by symbol and Ensembl ID (ENSG…); NCBI's files use the numeric Entrez GeneID. I exported an Ensembl-to-Entrez table from Ensembl BioMart for all human genes and merged it into the SFARI table with pd.merge(…, on="ensembl-id"). 1,091 genes mapped.

Under the hood

Symbols are a trap: they get renamed, and the same symbol can mean different genes in different species. Stable IDs are the only safe join key, and a chunk of real bioinformatics work is exactly this kind of ID plumbing. Where one Ensembl ID maps to two Entrez IDs (it happens), the inner join keeps both rows, which is the right call for annotation counting.

Pull the Gene Ontology annotations

gene2go.gz is a 100+ MB file covering every species NCBI annotates. I downloaded it with urllib, read it with pandas and merged it on GeneID, which gives one row per gene per GO term.

Keep only curated evidence

Every annotation carries an evidence code. I filtered to TAS, "traceable author statement", meaning a curator attached the term to the gene from a specific published source. That drops the electronically inferred annotations, which are more numerous but much noisier.

Under the hood

There are about twenty GO evidence codes. IEA (inferred from electronic annotation) is the bulk of the file; EXP/IDA/IMP are direct experiments; TAS and NAS are statements in papers. Choosing TAS was a deliberate trade: fewer annotations per gene, but every one of them has a paper behind it.

Split by score and count terms

Three DataFrames, one per gene score. For each, value_counts on the GO term column gives the ten most common annotations.

Classify with PANTHER

I wrote each tier's Entrez IDs to a text file, uploaded them to PANTHER, ran its functional classification with the Biological Process ontology and exported the counts. The chart below is drawn from those three exports.

Under the hood

PANTHER also has a REST API, and the notebook called it: /services/oai/pantherdb/enrich/overrep with the gene list, organism 9606 (human), the GO Biological Process data set and Fisher's test with FDR correction. The JSON that comes back has a result per term with expected count, fold enrichment, p-value and FDR, which I sorted by FDR and printed for terms under 0.05. Same analysis as the website, but repeatable.

What I concluded

The gene score tells you how confident SFARI is, not what kind of gene it is. If the tiers had sorted genes by biology (say, all the synaptic genes in tier 1) the PANTHER bars would differ; they don't. That pushed me toward Part 3: if function doesn't separate the genes, maybe interaction structure does.

Part 3

Do the proteins work together?

Genes don't act alone; their proteins bind, modify and regulate each other. STRING is a database of known and predicted protein-protein interactions, scored by how much evidence supports each one. If autism genes were a random grab-bag, their proteins would interact about as often as any random set of human proteins. If they share biology, they should interact far more.

Build the network from the score-1 genes

I uploaded the 215 score-1 Entrez IDs to STRING, species Homo sapiens, default confidence. STRING resolved 213 of them to proteins and drew 1,555 interactions between them.

Read the statistics

STRING's analysis panel gives the numbers that matter: average node degree 14.6 (each protein interacts with about fifteen others in the set), average clustering coefficient 0.44, and an expected edge count of 461 for a random set of the same size. Observed 1,555 against expected 461 is a PPI-enrichment p-value below 10⁻¹⁶.

Cluster it

STRING offers MCL clustering (Markov clustering: random walks on the graph that tend to stay inside dense regions). With the default inflation setting it carved the network into clusters; I exported the TSV and took the two largest, 33 and 26 proteins.

Under the hood

MCL alternates two operations on the interaction matrix: expansion (multiply the matrix by itself, which spreads flow along paths) and inflation (raise entries to a power and renormalise, which sharpens strong flows and kills weak ones). Repeat until it stops changing; the surviving flows define the clusters. One parameter, the inflation power, controls how fine the clusters are.

Ask what each cluster does

Each cluster's IDs went back into PANTHER, this time with the Pathway ontology rather than Biological Process, to see whether the two groups sit on different pathways.

Do it again in Python

To make the analysis repeatable I rebuilt the network in code: STRING's API converts Entrez IDs to STRING IDs, then returns the interaction table; networkx turns that into a graph; and GSEApy's enrichr runs a KEGG pathway enrichment on the genes ranked by network importance.

Under the hood

The STRING API needs two calls because its interaction endpoint only accepts a handful of free-text identifiers at a time; converting to internal IDs first lifts that limit. Identifiers are joined with %0D (an encoded carriage return) in the URL.

ids = "%0D".join(map(str, score1_entrez_ids))
string_ids = fetch("https://string-db.org/api/tsv-no-header/get_string_ids?identifiers=" + ids + "&format=only-ids")
table = fetch("https://string-db.org/api/tsv/network?identifiers=" + "%0D".join(string_ids) + "&species=9606")

G = nx.Graph()
G.add_edges_from(zip(table.preferredName_A, table.preferredName_B))

ranked = [g for g, _ in sorted(G.degree(), key=lambda kv: kv[1], reverse=True)]
enr = gseapy.enrichr(gene_list=ranked, gene_sets=["KEGG_2021_Human"], organism="human")

I ranked the genes three ways, by degree, by closeness centrality and by clustering coefficient, and got the same top pathways each time. That is not a coincidence: Enrichr runs an over-representation test on the set of genes and ignores their order, so the ranking only matters if you cut the list. A good reminder to read what a tool actually computes.

The two clusters

Cluster 1 · 33 proteins

Where neurons talk to each other

Neurexins and neuroligins (NRXN1-3, NLGN2-4X) are the adhesion molecules that hold a synapse together; SHANK2, DLG4 and SYNGAP1 are the scaffold behind the receiving side; GRIN1/2A/2B are glutamate-receptor subunits; GABRB3 and SLC6A1 handle inhibitory signalling; STXBP1, SYN1 and RIMS1 release the vesicles. Almost the whole cluster is the machinery of a synapse.

Cluster 2 · 26 proteins

Where genes are switched on and off

CHD3/7, SMARCA2/4, ARID1B and SRCAP are chromatin remodellers; CREBBP, EP300, KMT2A/C and DNMT3A write chemical marks on histones and DNA; CTCF, YY1, MEF2C and SATB1 are transcription factors; MED13/13L are part of the Mediator complex. This is the machinery that decides which genes a developing neuron expresses.

This split is the headline result of the project. The strong-evidence autism genes are not scattered across cell biology; they concentrate in two systems, synaptic communication and the regulation of gene expression during brain development. That matches what the wider literature has converged on, and here it fell out of the data on its own.

Pathway enrichment

The PANTHER pathway runs on the clusters were thin, because PANTHER recognised only a handful of the identifiers in each export (6 of 33 and 5 of 26). What it did find fits the picture: glutamate-receptor and acetylcholine-receptor signalling for cluster 1; Wnt, TGF-β, p53 and bZIP transcription-factor pathways for cluster 2; and Huntington disease in both, which is the pathway database's name for a set of neuronal survival and transcription genes.

Discussion

What it adds up to

  • Evidence is concentrated. A few genes (SHANK3, MECP2, NRXN1) carry most of the literature, and that literature has grown every year since about 2007. The score-3 tier is barely studied at all.
  • Function doesn't separate the tiers. GO and PANTHER profiles for score 1, 2 and 3 are near-identical at the broad level. The SFARI score measures confidence, not biology.
  • Interaction structure does separate the genes. The score-1 proteins interact more than three times as often as chance predicts, and they divide into a synaptic cluster and a chromatin-regulation cluster.
  • Tools disagree, and that's information. PANTHER's website, PANTHER's API, STRING and Enrichr each gave a slightly different view of the same genes, mostly because of identifier mapping and background sets. Reporting which tool, which ontology and which background was used is part of the result.
If I did it again

I would run the network analysis on all three tiers and on the union, use the Entrez-ID lists for the PANTHER pathway step so every gene maps, and try KEGG and Reactome alongside PANTHER. I would also replace the website clicks with API calls end to end, so the whole project is one notebook that reruns from the raw download.

Earlier in the course

Warm-up: the gene humans lost

The first coursework was a sequence-level exercise that taught the Biopython plumbing the project relied on. The subject was GULO, the gene for the enzyme that makes the last step of vitamin C. Most mammals have a working copy; humans, other apes and guinea pigs carry only a broken one, which is why we have to eat our vitamin C and they don't.

Find the reference sequence

Entrez.esearch against the nucleotide database with a targeted query, GULO[Gene] AND Mus musculus[Organism] AND mRNA[Filter] AND RefSeq[Filter], then efetch each hit as a GenBank record and keep the curated NM_ accession over the predicted XM_ one.

Translate it

A GenBank record lists its features; the CDS feature marks the coding sequence. Biopython translates that region into the 440-amino-acid enzyme in one call.

Hunt for it in other genomes

Remote BLAST through NCBIWWW.qblast: blastn with the mRNA, blastp with the protein, and tblastx restricted to one species at a time with a taxonomy-ID filter (txid9606[ORGN] for human). I ran it for human, the frog Xenopus tropicalis, the Asian elephant, the guinea pig and the lancelet Branchiostoma, and tabulated score, coverage, identity and E-value for each hit.

Under the hood

The three flavours answer different questions. blastn finds similar DNA, which fades quickly over evolutionary time. blastp compares proteins, which are conserved far longer. tblastx translates both query and database in all six reading frames, so it can find a protein-level match even in a genome that has lost the annotated gene, which is exactly how you detect a pseudogene: the pieces are still there, but with stops and frameshifts where the working enzyme used to be.

Looking back

What it taught me

  1. Identifiers are the real work. Symbol, Ensembl gene, Entrez gene, STRING protein, UniProt: every tool speaks a different one, and most lost results in this project trace back to a mapping that silently dropped rows. Stable IDs and inner joins with row counts before and after.
  2. Know what the statistic assumes. Enrichment p-values depend entirely on the background set and on whether the tool uses your ranking. Three "different" analyses gave identical output because the tool ignored the thing I was varying.
  3. Scripts over clicks. Everything done on a website had to be redone when the list changed. The parts that went through APIs reran in seconds. Reproducibility is a feature you build in from the first cell.
  4. Let structure speak. The most convincing result was not a p-value but the two clusters: a list of 33 gene names that any neuroscientist would recognise as a synapse. Good network analysis makes biology visible rather than just significant.
Reference

Key terms

SFARI gene score
SFARI's 1–3 confidence rating for an autism gene. 1 is strongest; typically three or more independent de novo gene-disrupting mutations.
de novo mutation
A mutation present in a child but in neither parent, so it arose new. Strong evidence when it recurs in the same gene across unrelated patients.
Entrez
NCBI's shared search layer over PubMed, Gene, Nucleotide, Taxonomy and the rest. Biopython wraps it.
Gene Ontology (GO)
A controlled vocabulary describing where a gene product is, what it does and which processes it joins. gene2go links genes to terms.
evidence code / TAS
Why a GO annotation exists. TAS, traceable author statement, means a curator took it from a specific paper.
PANTHER
A gene-function classification and enrichment tool with its own ontologies for processes and pathways.
STRING
A database of protein-protein interactions, each scored by experimental, database, text-mining and other evidence.
node degree
How many interactions a protein has in the network. High degree marks a hub.
MCL
Markov clustering: simulates flow on the graph and cuts where flow is weak. Finds dense groups without being told how many.
enrichment
A test of whether a pathway appears in a gene list more often than chance, given a background of all genes.
BLAST
Fast local alignment search. blastn for DNA, blastp for protein, tblastx for translated-vs-translated.
pseudogene
A gene that has lost its function through mutation but is still recognisable in the genome.
