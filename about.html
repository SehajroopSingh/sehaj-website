About

AI is cheap to demo and expensive to trust.

The hard part was never the model. It's the data an organization already has, the systems the AI has to live inside, the budget it has to fit, and the people on both sides of it — employees and customers — who have to trust it enough to stop checking its work.

That's what decides whether any of the efficiency actually shows up. It's also the part I work on: I've built it inside an FDA-regulated MedTech enterprise, as the solo engineer behind a learning platform with real users, and I teach it to the people who'll have to live with these systems next.

Engineer by trade. Founder by conviction. Teacher by choice.

The deeper lens

Evidence before belief

I didn't start in software. I started pre-med, working shifts in the emergency department and on the oncology floor at UCLA's Ronald Reagan Medical Center, then as a neuroscience major. What stayed with me from those years wasn't the biology. It was the method. Medicine doesn't trust a treatment because it sounds right or because the pitch was convincing; it trusts it because it survived a controlled experiment against real outcomes. Every protocol on that floor was a hypothesis someone had tested.

That was the part I couldn't put down. Experiments, probability, the discipline of asking "how would we know?" guided me from medicine into statistics at UC Santa Barbara. Statistics gave me the mathematics under the method; the natural next question was what happens to it in practice — how it gets applied, and how the machines running it actually work. That curiosity became a master's in computer science at Georgia Tech, where I learned to build the systems those experiments run on. The question never changed, only the tools. I'm less interested in whether a system is clever than in whether we can show it works.

Every turn in that path has pointed back at education. My own route through it — community college before a UC, then a master's — is why I built MemSurf: I wanted to put that same discipline into how people learn, and building it alone, for real users, was the most honest test I could run. No team to hide behind, no demo day, just whether students came back. It's also why I teach computer science part-time at Cal Poly Pomona.

Teaching is the most validating, soul-filling work I do, and I hope to keep it as a side career for the rest of my life. It's how I give back: taking what I'm learning on the job and handing it to students a few steps behind me on the same route. I'm grateful to have the chance, this early in my career, to get good at it.

I've run that experiment solo. The next step is to run it with a team, at the scale only an organization can reach, on a problem I'd be glad to spend years on. That's the work I want next.

Method

How I work

I start with the workflow, not the model.

Shadow the job first. The value is usually in a step nobody has looked at closely, and the model choice comes last.

I write the eval before the prompt.

Real inputs, ideal outputs, hand-checked. Until that exists, “it works” is an opinion.

I build the smallest system that passes.

Prompt before RAG, RAG before agents. Every layer you add is one you maintain, debug and pay for after launch.

I score cost and latency alongside quality.

A system that is right but slow or expensive gets quietly turned off.

I treat the existing stack as the spec.

The AI has to live inside their systems, permissions and compliance rules, or it doesn’t live.

I explain it until a non-engineer can defend it.

If the people who own the workflow can’t say why the system is trustworthy, it isn’t yet.

Questions, answered

FAQ

What do you actually build?

Agentic LLM pipelines, retrieval systems, evaluation harnesses and guardrails — the parts that turn a demo into something an organization can run. I’ve shipped them inside a Fortune-50 MedTech enterprise under FDA constraints, built a Graph-RAG system for FotonVR, and built MemSurf end to end as its only engineer. Alongside that, I teach computer science at Cal Poly Pomona.

Why forward-deployed work?

Because the problems that kill AI projects are discovered sitting next to the user, not in a backlog. The data isn’t where the diagram says, the “simple” workflow has six exceptions, the approver nobody mentioned has a veto. I’d rather find that out in week one, in the room, and build to it. I also like being accountable for whether the thing gets used, not just whether it shipped.

How do you build AI in a regulated environment?

The same way, with the paperwork turned up. A gold standard built from real inputs, every change scored against it, a trail from each output back to its source, and guardrails on what the system is allowed to say. At J&J MedTech that meant a generative-AI system built over the training material the organization already had, held to FDA-regulated standards. Regulation is a forcing function for the discipline you should have anyway.

Why do you still teach?

Because students find the hole in an explanation within minutes, and the hole in the explanation is usually the hole in the system. It’s a part-time commitment — a section a term alongside full-time work — and I plan to keep it that size.

What’s happening with MemSurf?

It’s live, with 100+ active users, and I’ve built and run it as the only engineer. It’s where I’ve tested every part of my process end to end, from a pipeline that failed constantly to one that runs at 99%+ reliability. It isn’t a competing commitment: I’m looking for a full-time team, and MemSurf runs on my own time.

What’s your stack?

Python end to end: Django and Celery for the application and pipeline layer, Vertex AI for models, retrieval over a vector store, evaluation harnesses I’ve written myself, and guardrails on inputs and outputs. I pick tools by what the organization already runs, not by what’s newest.

Why statistics before computer science?

Statistics gave me the mathematics under the question I care about: how do we know this works? Computer science gave me the rest — how that mathematics gets applied and how the machines running it actually work. One without the other produces either a model nobody can ship or a system nobody can trust.

What are you looking for?

An AI engineer or forward-deployed engineer role on a team that ships to real users, based in Southern California, on a problem worth several years.
